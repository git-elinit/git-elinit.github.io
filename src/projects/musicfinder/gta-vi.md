## A playlist already existed

I wanted the GTA VI soundtrack in one place. Then I found that the [official Deluxe album already had a perfectly usable 57-track list](https://www.rockstargames.com/VI/music). I could have opened the playlist and been done.

Instead, I remembered MusicFinder. Apparently, once you've built a music experiment, every new list of songs looks like a reason to open it again.

## One more job for my old experiment

The original MusicFinder task was about reading songs from screenshots of my library. This one was different. I asked Codex, working in the MusicFinder workspace, to research the Deluxe album and give me a plain-text file with direct YouTube Music links for its individual tracks.

That distinction matters. The screenshot-extraction program didn't suddenly discover the GTA VI soundtrack. I was using Codex's broader research and extraction abilities in the same project workspace.

I also had to be specific about the output. I wanted watch links, not search pages, and one URL per line. A playlist would have been simpler. Naturally, I requested a text file instead.

## 57 songs, one text file

Rockstar lists 34 tracks on the original album and 23 more on the Deluxe edition. The export contained 57 distinct YouTube Music watch URLs, one per line. It covered that album, not every song on GTA VI's in-game radio stations.

It was exactly the kind of tidy output I had asked for. It was also not the same thing as listening to 57 recordings and confirming each match. The file showed completeness in format; it didn't settle every question about accuracy.

## Was this necessary?

No. There was already a playlist.

I had effectively asked an agent to turn a ready-made album into a more inconvenient format, just because I could. :))

But I liked seeing what happened when I gave an old experiment a new task. MusicFinder began with a practical problem. This time, curiosity was the problem—and maybe not even a problem at all.

Will I use these 57 links for something else? Maybe. They're already collected in a format I can take somewhere else: another playlist, a different tool, or another little experiment. I don't know what I'll do with them yet.

So perhaps the text file wasn't completely unnecessary after all. :))

## What I learned

The useful part wasn't claiming I'd built a better way to listen to GTA VI. I hadn't. It was noticing how much the result depended on the request: which album, how many tracks, what kind of links, and what the finished file should look like.

I could check that there were 57 lines and that each line had the shape of a direct YouTube Music URL. I couldn't honestly say the run had verified every recording by listening. That distinction is part of working with agents, too: define an output, check what you can, and name what remains uncertain.

Sometimes the unnecessary experiment is the one that makes the lesson easier to see.
